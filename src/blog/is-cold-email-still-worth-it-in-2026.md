You know the email. It begins: "Hi there, I was really impressed by your work at [COMPANY_NAME]."

Everyone with an inbox gets a version of it most days. We delete them without reading, the way you walk past a person holding a clipboard on the street.

And yet. Every so often an email arrives from a stranger who has clearly looked at what you do, noticed something specific, and asked a short question worth answering. You reply to those. Nearly everyone does.

Both of those are "cold email". Only one of them is dead.

So yes, cold email is still worth it in 2026, on one condition: it has to be worth receiving. The cheap, high-volume version was killed by two things at once. Mailbox providers got strict, and AI made generic outreach so abundant that people stopped reading it. The careful, low-volume version works as well as ever, possibly better, because so little of it exists.

I am an engineer, not a salesperson, but I have been on both sides of this. The engineering view helps, because cold email today is partly a deliverability system with rules.

## What changed

**The rules became enforceable.** In February 2024, Google and Yahoo began requiring bulk senders to authenticate their mail with SPF, DKIM and DMARC, offer one-click unsubscribe, and keep spam complaints low. Google's guidance is to stay under a 0.3 percent complaint rate and ideally well below it. Microsoft introduced comparable requirements for Outlook in 2025. Before this, poor practice lowered your results. Now it gets your domain throttled or blocked.

**AI removed the cost of personalization, and its value.** A first line that mentions the recipient's latest post used to signal effort. Now it is generated by the thousand, and everyone recognizes it. Shallow personalization has become a negative signal.

**Inboxes filter harder.** Providers increasingly sort and summarize mail automatically. A message has to pass a machine's judgment before a person ever sees it.

**Volume economics broke.** Benchmarks published by outreach vendors typically put average reply rates around 3 to 4 percent, and those figures count every reply, including "please remove me". The response to that has been to send more, which raises complaints and lowers deliverability further.

The mechanism behind all four is the same. Mailbox providers watch how recipients react to a sender. If enough people delete or report your mail, the provider stops delivering it, including the messages people would have wanted. So the only strategy that survives is fewer emails, sent to people with a reason to want them.

## Why it is still worth doing

- **It is the only channel where you choose who hears from you.** Content and ads wait for people to arrive. Outreach lets you pick the fifty accounts that matter.
- **It is fast feedback.** Twenty good emails tell you within a week whether your message means anything to your market.
- **It costs almost nothing** compared with paid acquisition.
- **Decision-makers do read email.** They just do not read bad email.
- **The bar is low.** When most outreach is obviously automated, one that is clearly written by a person about a real problem stands out.

## The version that works

**Start with the list, not the copy.** Most of the result is decided by who you write to. A tight list of people who demonstrably have the problem beats a broad list with a clever message.

**Use a real trigger.** The best reason to write is something that happened: they are hiring for a role your product replaces, they launched in a market you serve, they posted publicly about the problem. If there is no reason to write to this person this week, you probably should not.

**Keep it short and plain.** Under a hundred words. No images, no tracking-heavy formatting, minimal links. It should look like a message one person typed to another, because it should be one.

**Say something only you could say.** An observation about their product, a specific result you got for a similar company, a concrete idea. If the sentence could be sent to a thousand people unchanged, cut it.

**Ask for something small.** Not a thirty-minute demo. A question they can answer in one line, or permission to send something useful.

**Follow up twice, with new information.** Not "bumping this". A second angle or a relevant example. Then stop.

A structure I find works:

> One line on why you are writing to them specifically, based on a real trigger.
> One line on the problem you think they have.
> One line of evidence that you have solved it.
> One small question.

## The technical basics you cannot skip

Treat this part as infrastructure. It is not optional.

- **Authenticate properly.** SPF, DKIM and DMARC set up and aligned for the sending domain.
- **Protect your main domain.** Many teams send outreach from a separate, closely related domain so a mistake does not harm delivery of invoices and product mail.
- **Warm up gradually.** A new mailbox that sends hundreds of messages on day one looks like spam, because it usually is.
- **Verify addresses.** High bounce rates damage reputation quickly.
- **Make leaving easy.** A clear way to opt out, honored immediately.
- **Watch complaint rates** in the providers' own postmaster tools, not only in your sending tool's dashboard.
- **Know the law where your recipients are.** The rules for unsolicited commercial email differ considerably between the US, the EU, the UK and other regions. Check before you send.

## Where AI helps and where it hurts

AI is very good at the research: summarizing a company, finding the trigger, drafting a first pass, checking tone. Use it for that.

It hurts when it writes and sends without a person deciding that this message, to this recipient, is worth sending. The moment output looks generated, the recipient assumes they are one of ten thousand, and they are usually right.

## When cold email is the wrong tool

- **Low-priced consumer or self-serve products.** The math rarely works. Content, community and product-led growth fit better.
- **Markets where everyone knows everyone.** A warm introduction beats any cold message.
- **When you do not know your customer yet.** Outreach amplifies a message. It will not find one for you.
- **When you cannot handle the replies.** A slow or careless response to an interested reply wastes the whole effort.

## Channels that pair well with it

Cold email works best as one part of a small system:

- **Public writing** so that when a recipient looks you up, they find evidence you know the subject. See [why engineers should write in public](/blog/why-engineers-should-write-in-public/).
- **Communities** where your customers already talk, which is how many successful companies found their [first customers](/blog/how-startups-get-their-first-customers/).
- **Warm paths.** A mutual connection, a comment exchange, a shared event.

## The verdict

Cold email in 2026 rewards the opposite of what the tools encourage. Send fewer messages to better-chosen people, with a real reason, from a properly configured domain, written by someone who would be comfortable receiving the same message. That standard is easy to describe and takes real work to meet, which is exactly why it still gets replies.
